Word Families and Affixes
A word family is a group of words that share the same base and are related in meaning, such as decide, decision, decisive and indecisive. Learning a whole family at once is more efficient than learning each word separately.
Most word families are built from a base word plus affixes. There are two kinds of affixes: prefixes, which come before the base, and suffixes, which come after it. For example, in the word unhappiness, un- is a prefix and -ness is a suffix.
Prefixes usually change the meaning but not the part of speech. The prefix un- often means not, as in unable, and re- means again, as in rewrite. The prefix pre- means before, as in preview, and mis- means wrongly, as in misunderstand.
Suffixes usually change the part of speech. The suffix -tion turns a verb into a noun, as in educate and education. The suffix -ly turns an adjective into an adverb, as in quick and quickly, and -able makes an adjective from a verb, as in comfortable or readable.
Researchers such as Paul Nation suggest that a learner who knows about 3,000 word families can understand roughly 95 percent of the words in an everyday text. Reaching 98 percent, which is considered comfortable for reading without a dictionary, requires about 8,000 to 9,000 word families.
A common mistake is to assume that every affix works with every base. The word informal takes the prefix in-, but the correct opposites of possible and legal are impossible and illegal, because the prefix changes its spelling before the letters p and l.
To practise, learners can choose a base word each day and write down as many family members as they can before checking a dictionary.
