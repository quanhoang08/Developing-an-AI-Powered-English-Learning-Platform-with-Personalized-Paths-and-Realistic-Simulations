Spaced Repetition and Vocabulary Retention
Learners forget new words quickly. In 1885 the German psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus measured this decay and described the forgetting curve: without review, most of what we learn is lost within a few days, and the loss is steepest in the first hours after learning.
Spaced repetition is a study technique that fights the forgetting curve by scheduling each review at a longer interval than the last. A word you remember well is shown again after a long gap, while a word you struggle with returns soon. This way the effort is spent where it is most needed, and a student can retain thousands of words with only a few minutes of review each day.
The most widely used scheduling rule is the SM-2 algorithm, created by Piotr Wozniak in 1987 for the SuperMemo software. After each review the learner grades their recall on a scale from 0 to 5, where 0 means a complete blackout and 5 means a perfect, effortless answer. Every item has an ease factor that starts at 2.5 and moves up or down depending on the grades the learner gives.
The intervals in SM-2 follow a simple pattern. The first successful review happens one day after learning, the second review happens six days later, and after that the previous interval is multiplied by the ease factor. If the learner gives a grade below 3, the item is treated as forgotten: its repetition count is reset and it returns to a one-day interval.
An older, paper-based method is the Leitner system, proposed by the German science journalist Sebastian Leitner in the 1970s. Flashcards are kept in five boxes. A card moves up one box each time it is answered correctly and is reviewed less often, while a wrong answer sends it back to the first box. The first box is reviewed every day and the last box only every few weeks.
Spaced repetition works best when words are learned in context. Studies of second language learners show that seeing a word inside a meaningful sentence, rather than as an isolated translation pair, improves long-term retention because the brain links the word with its situation and grammar. For this reason good flashcards include an example sentence and the source where the word was found.
Sleep also matters. Memory consolidation takes place during sleep, so reviewing new words in the evening and again the next morning is more effective than a single long session. Experts generally recommend adding only a limited number of new words per day, often between ten and twenty, because every new word creates future reviews that must be completed.
Finally, consistency beats intensity. A learner who reviews for ten minutes every day will remember more after a month than a learner who studies for three hours once a week. Skipping days is the most common reason that review queues grow too large and motivation drops.
